Dark Side · March 2026

$3 Million.
3,000 Stolen Identities.
One Gambling App.

Two 29-year-olds from Glastonbury, Connecticut turned FanDuel's new-user bonus system into a $3 million ATM — hitting the same play 3,000 times with stolen identities. A state gaming investigator spotted the pattern. A 45-count federal indictment followed.

📅 March 25, 2026 ✍️ NMD ZAZA ⏰ 6 min read

They found the exploit and ran it 3,000 times.

Amitoj Kapoor and Sidharth Lillaney, both 29, both from the quiet suburbs of Glastonbury, Connecticut, had figured something out. FanDuel, like most online gambling platforms, offers promotional bonuses to new users when they sign up for the first time. Create an account, deposit, claim your bonus, cash out. Simple.

The problem — for them — is that you can only do it once per person. You only get to be new once.

Unless you have 3,000 other people's identities.

According to federal prosecutors, Kapoor and Lillaney obtained the real Social Security numbers, names, and personal information of approximately 3,000 individuals. With those identities in hand, they ran a methodical operation: open a fraudulent bank account or virtual stored-value card in the victim's name, create a new FanDuel account, collect the new-user bonus, withdraw the funds, repeat.

They ran this play an estimated 3,000 times. Total take: approximately $3 million.

⚠ Scale of the Operation

3,000 stolen identities. 3,000 fraudulent bank accounts and virtual cards opened. 3,000 fake FanDuel registrations. The operation was not opportunistic — it was systematic. This was an industrial-scale identity theft ring running out of Connecticut's suburbs.

The scheme in detail — how it actually worked.

Prosecutors laid out the mechanics in the indictment. The scheme had three layers:

Layer 1 — Identity Acquisition

Kapoor and Lillaney obtained the personal identifying information of real individuals — names, Social Security numbers, dates of birth, addresses. The indictment does not specify where or how this data was sourced. Federal prosecutors charged identity fraud, meaning the PII was used without the victims' knowledge or consent.

Layer 2 — Financial Infrastructure

For each stolen identity, the pair opened fraudulent bank accounts and obtained virtual stored-value cards — prepaid digital accounts — in the victims' names. These accounts served a dual purpose: they provided the banking rails needed to fund a FanDuel account, and they created a financial buffer between the fraud and the perpetrators' actual finances.

Layer 3 — The Bonus Harvest

With each fraudulent identity paired to a financial account, Kapoor and Lillaney created a new FanDuel user account and collected the new-user promotional bonus. The funds were deposited, the bonus claimed, then withdrawn back to the fraudulent bank account or virtual card — before routing further. Three thousand times. Roughly $1,000 per cycle on average.

"The scheme generated approximately $3,000,000 in fraudulent proceeds, affecting approximately 3,000 victims whose identities were used without authorization."

The one detail that unraveled everything.

Gaming fraud is a surprisingly well-monitored space. Online gambling platforms are licensed, regulated, and required to report suspicious activity — and state gaming regulators actively analyze transaction patterns across platforms.

That's exactly how Kapoor and Lillaney got caught.

An investigator with the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection Gaming Division noticed an anomaly: an unusual concentration of new FanDuel user accounts linked to the same patterns — similar funding structures, similar bonus-claim behavior, cycling at scale. The behavior didn't look like thousands of individual new users. It looked like one operation running the same play repeatedly.

DCP gaming investigators don't have federal arrest authority. But they knew who to call. They contacted the IRS Criminal Investigation Division — the financial crimes arm of the IRS — and handed off the pattern data.

IRS-CI built the federal case. On February 6, 2026, Amitoj Kapoor and Sidharth Lillaney were arrested and arraigned in U.S. District Court in New Haven, Connecticut.

💡 The Paper Trail

Money laundering through virtual stored-value cards and fraudulent bank accounts leaves a digital trail that IRS Criminal Investigation is specifically trained to follow. Every account opening, every deposit, every withdrawal generates records. The investigators had 3,000 data points to work with.

45 counts. Federal court. What they're facing.

The grand jury returned a 45-count indictment covering the full range of federal charges prosecutors could bring against the pair:

Charge Category Federal Statute Max Exposure
Conspiracy to Commit Wire Fraud 18 U.S.C. § 1349 20 years
Wire Fraud 18 U.S.C. § 1343 20 years/count
Aggravated Identity Theft 18 U.S.C. § 1028A Mandatory 2 years
Money Laundering 18 U.S.C. § 1956 20 years/count

Aggravated identity theft under 18 U.S.C. § 1028A carries a mandatory 2-year consecutive sentence on top of any other sentence imposed — it cannot be served concurrently and cannot be waived by the judge. With 45 counts spread across wire fraud, identity fraud, and money laundering, the total theoretical exposure runs into decades.

Both defendants were arraigned February 6, 2026 in U.S. District Court, New Haven. As of March 2026, the case is actively being prosecuted by the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Connecticut. No guilty pleas have been publicly announced.

The 3,000 victims — and what happened to them.

The Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection sent identity theft notices to over 1,200 affected individuals identified through the investigation. The remaining victims are still being located and notified.

For those 3,000 people, the damage varies. The most direct impact: fraudulent bank accounts and virtual cards were opened in their names without their knowledge. Depending on how the scheme was executed, victims may face:

  • 1
    ChexSystems flags from fraudulent bank account openings. Banks report to ChexSystems when accounts are closed for suspicious activity. Victims may find themselves unable to open new bank accounts because a fraudulent account opened in their name was later flagged and closed.
  • 2
    Hard inquiries from financial account applications. Opening bank accounts and virtual stored-value cards may generate inquiries on credit files or specialty consumer reports. Victims may have unexplained inquiry activity they never authorized.
  • 3
    Ongoing risk from exposed PII. The personal identifying information that was used in this scheme — SSNs, dates of birth, addresses — remains compromised. Victims whose data was used here are at risk for future fraud schemes using the same information.

What You Should Know

  • •
    New-user bonus fraud is a known attack vector. This scheme exploited a structural vulnerability in online gambling's promotional economy. Every platform that offers new-user incentives is a potential target for this type of identity-fueled bonus harvesting at scale.
  • •
    State gaming regulators — not banks — spotted this. The DCP Gaming Division identified the pattern before any financial institution flagged it. This case shows that industry-specific regulators can catch fraud that general financial monitoring misses.
  • •
    Virtual stored-value cards are not anonymous. The defendants used virtual prepaid cards to layer the proceeds. IRS-CI traces these routinely — every card issuance, load, and withdrawal is logged and subpoena-able.
  • •
    1,200+ victims have been notified — 1,800 more are still being located. If you're in Connecticut and received an identity theft notice from DCP in early 2026, this case is the source. Check your ChexSystems report and specialty credit files immediately.

Updates

The case is being prosecuted by the U.S. Attorney's Office, District of Connecticut. Both defendants — Amitoj Kapoor and Sidharth Lillaney — were arraigned February 6, 2026. No trial date has been publicly set. The IRS Criminal Investigation Division and the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection are the lead investigating agencies.

FanDuel has not publicly commented on the case. The DCP investigation that cracked this scheme originated from gaming transaction monitoring — the same monitoring that online gambling platforms are required by state law to maintain.
