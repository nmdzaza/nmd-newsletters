Dark Side · PPP Fraud · December 2025

She Was a Phoenix News Anchor.
He Was a Tech Founder.
Together They Stole $63 Million.

Nathan Reis and Stephanie Hockridge founded Blueacorn in April 2020 — a fintech company that processed over $1 billion in pandemic relief loans. Sixty-three million dollars of it was fraudulent. They moved to Puerto Rico. The FBI found them anyway. Both are now doing 10 years in federal prison.

📅 December 2025 ✍️ NMD ZAZA ⏰ 6 min read

A pandemic, a fintech company, and $63 million in forged documents.

In April 2020, as millions of small business owners scrambled to keep their doors open, Nathan Reis and his wife Stephanie Hockridge — a former on-air anchor at Phoenix's KNXV-TV — launched a company called Blueacorn. The pitch was simple: Blueacorn would help small businesses navigate the Paycheck Protection Program. The SBA had just opened the floodgates on $800 billion in pandemic relief loans, and the application process was complicated. Blueacorn was there to help.

What the SBA didn't know was that "help" meant something very specific to Reis and Hockridge. It meant fabricating payroll records. Forging tax documents and bank statements. Coaching applicants on how to falsify their own numbers to qualify for larger loans. And charging those applicants a percentage fee on every fraudulent dollar they pocketed — a kickback structure they built right into the business model.

By 2021, Blueacorn had processed nearly three times as many PPP loans as major national banks combined — more than $1 billion in taxpayer-guaranteed funds flowing through a company that hadn't existed 12 months earlier. It was, on paper, one of the great fintech success stories of the pandemic era. The reality was a $63 million fraud operation running at industrial scale.

The VIPPP program — fraud as a concierge service

Most of the fraudulent activity ran through a private, invite-only tier Hockridge built inside Blueacorn called "VIPPP." It wasn't advertised publicly. You had to be referred in.

Under VIPPP, Hockridge and her co-conspirators recruited referral agents who would bring in borrowers looking for larger-than-eligible loans. Those agents would then walk applicants through exactly how to falsify their applications — what numbers to use, what documents to submit, what a convincing fake payroll record looked like. The goal was simple: get the biggest loan the SBA would approve, charge a percentage on the back end, and repeat.

Prosecutors documented over 530 fraudulent loan applications that Reis, Hockridge, and their network processed. Each one carried fabricated documentation. Each one was backed by the federal government. And each one came with a fee — a cut of the fraudulent proceeds that Blueacorn pocketed before passing the rest to the applicant.

⚠ The Scale of the Operation

Blueacorn processed over $1 billion in PPP loans total. Sixty-three million dollars of that — across 530+ documented fraudulent applications — was obtained through fabricated payroll records, forged tax documents, and falsified bank statements. The fraud wasn't a side hustle. It was the business.

"[Reis] orchestrated one of the nation's largest schemes to defraud" the Paycheck Protection Program during the pandemic."

— Acting Attorney General Matthew R. Galeotti, December 2025

The exit strategy — loan forgiveness and Puerto Rico

After the money was in, Reis and Hockridge did what every sophisticated fraud operator does: they made sure the trail went cold. They applied for and received PPP loan forgiveness on the fraudulent loans — converting a short-term note into a grant, effectively closing the books on the liability. Then they left Arizona entirely and relocated to Puerto Rico, where favorable tax laws and distance from federal investigators gave them a comfortable buffer.

It wasn't enough. The FBI, IRS Criminal Investigation, Federal Reserve/CFPB Office of Inspector General, and the SBA's own Office of Inspector General had been working the case for years. The documentation trail was extensive — 530 fraudulent applications across hundreds of borrowers leaves a lot of paper. Investigators methodically reconstructed the scheme: the forged documents, the referral network, the fee structure, the communications between Hockridge and her VIPPP agents coaching applicants on how to lie.

Trial, plea, and sentencing

Hockridge went to trial first. In June 2025, a federal jury in Fort Worth, Texas convicted her on one count of conspiracy to commit wire fraud, acquitting her on four additional wire fraud counts. On November 21, 2025, a federal judge sentenced her to 120 months — 10 years — at Federal Prison Camp Bryan, Texas, with two years of supervised release to follow. She surrendered on December 30, 2025 at 2:00 PM.

Reis took a different path. In August 2025, he pleaded guilty to his role in the scheme. On December 18, 2025 — three weeks after his wife surrendered — Reis was sentenced to 10 years in federal prison. He was also ordered to pay over $66 million in restitution to the Small Business Administration, representing the full scope of fraudulent loans processed through Blueacorn.

The investigation was led by the FBI, IRS Criminal Investigation, the Federal Reserve Board/CFPB Office of Inspector General, the Special Inspector General for Pandemic Recovery, and the SBA Office of Inspector General. The DOJ's Fraud Section has now prosecuted over 200 defendants across 130+ PPP-related cases, seizing more than $78 million in fraudulent proceeds since the CARES Act's passage.

What You Should Know

  • 1
    The forgiveness application was the closing move. Obtaining PPP loan forgiveness didn't erase the fraud — it converted it into an additional federal charge. Every fraudulent loan that was forgiven became documented evidence of a completed theft from the U.S. Treasury. Forgiveness was traceable, timestamped, and tied to the original fraudulent application.
  • 2
    The referral network left a paper trail prosecutors exploited fully. Every VIPPP referral agent who coached a borrower, received a cut, or submitted fabricated documentation became a potential cooperating witness. Building a fraud network gives investigators multiple entry points. The more people you bring in, the more people can flip.
  • 3
    Moving to Puerto Rico didn't create jurisdictional protection. PPP fraud is a federal crime prosecuted in the district where the applications were processed. Blueacorn operated out of the Northern District of Texas and other federal districts. Hockridge and Reis were within federal reach regardless of where they lived. Relocation buys time, not immunity.
  • 4
    530 fraudulent applications across $63 million is an operational footprint, not a mistake. This was a system — with intake agents, document fabrication, a coaching pipeline, and a fee structure. Prosecutors proved the conspiracy charge precisely because the evidence showed an organized, deliberate, and sustained operation, not isolated errors. The sentence reflects that.

Updates and related cases

The broader DOJ pandemic fraud enforcement push continues in 2026. The department has publicly stated that prosecutions for COVID-era fraud — including PPP, EIDL, and unemployment insurance schemes — remain an active enforcement priority, with no statute of limitations expiration imminent for many categories of federal fraud.

Co-conspirators identified as VIPPP referral agents and borrowers who knowingly submitted fraudulent applications remain under investigation in multiple federal districts. Additional charges have not been publicly ruled out.

Hockridge is currently serving her sentence at Federal Prison Camp Bryan, Texas. Reis is awaiting assignment to a federal facility following his December 2025 sentencing.
