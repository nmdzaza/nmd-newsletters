He Was a Nurse.
He Was Also Running a $420 Million Bank Fraud Scheme.

Matthew Thomas Onofrio coached 68 investors to lie to banks, inflated commercial real estate prices to the ceiling, and personally pocketed $35.7 million — all while working as a board-certified nurse anesthetist in Plymouth, Minnesota.

📅 December 15, 2025 ✍️ NMD ZAZA ⏰ 6 min read

The scheme was elegant. The investors never saw it coming.

Matthew Thomas Onofrio had a day job. He was a nurse anesthetist — licensed, employed, working in clinical settings. But he had built a second reputation as a real estate investment expert, one he carefully cultivated on the BiggerPockets podcast and LinkedIn networking groups where aspiring property investors went to learn.

Between 2020 and 2022, Onofrio ran what federal prosecutors described as a $420,564,795 bank fraud scheme — 68 deals, all of them built on coached deception. He didn't steal directly. He orchestrated.

Here's how it worked: Onofrio would enter into purchase agreements for commercial properties, then flip those agreements to inexperienced investors at heavily inflated prices. The investors couldn't afford the required 30% down payment — so Onofrio solved that problem for them. He coached them on exactly what to tell their banks. He helped them prepare falsified personal financial statements. And then he temporarily wired funds into their accounts to make it look like they had capital — only to pull the money back after the loan closed.

The investors believed they were making legitimate deals. The banks believed the financial statements. Onofrio stood in the middle, collecting assignment fees on inflated properties — and pocketing at least $35,745,252 before the whole structure collapsed under its own weight.

⚠ The Numbers

68 fraudulent commercial real estate loans. $420,564,795 in total fraudulently obtained bank financing. $35,745,252 personally netted by Onofrio. $5,398,641 in court-ordered restitution. Two years. One podcast. One license.

The BiggerPockets angle

BiggerPockets is one of the most well-known real estate investing communities in the country — a podcast, a forum, a platform where beginners and veterans exchange strategies. Onofrio used its credibility to position himself as a trustworthy expert. Investors who came to him weren't looking for a scam. They were looking for a mentor.

That's the architecture of how this kind of fraud operates. The victims weren't naive in any embarrassing sense — they were people who wanted to build wealth through real estate and found someone who seemed to have the blueprint. When that person starts walking you through loan applications and telling you what to write, you might not ask the hard questions. He had the reputation. He had the platform.

"Ringleader and architect."
— U.S. District Judge Susan Richard Nelson, at sentencing, December 11, 2025

How the investigators caught him

The unraveling started as investors began defaulting on their loans. Banks holding these commercial notes started comparing files and discovered a pattern: similar financial statement formats, similar funding sequences, properties assigned through the same intermediary at prices far above market. The paper trail pointed in one direction.

The IRS Criminal Investigation division, the FBI, and the FDIC's Office of Inspector General joined forces on the case. Federal investigators traced the temporary wire transfers in and out of investor accounts — the mechanism Onofrio used to fabricate the appearance of down payment capital. Those transactions left a clear forensic record.

The case moved to the District of Minnesota. Onofrio was charged with bank fraud under federal statutes carrying up to 30 years per count. He pleaded guilty.

The sentence and what it means

On December 11, 2025, Judge Susan Richard Nelson sentenced Onofrio to 36 months in federal prison followed by two years of supervised release. He was ordered to pay $5,398,641 in restitution.

For a scheme that touched $420 million in fraudulently obtained bank financing, a three-year sentence drew attention. Federal bank fraud statutes carry maximums of up to 30 years per count. Sentencing outcomes depend on numerous factors including cooperation, criminal history, guidelines calculations, and judicial discretion — details that weren't all made public in available court records.

The restitution figure — $5.4 million against $35.7 million in personal proceeds — means Onofrio kept the majority of what he pulled out of the scheme even after the court's order.

What You Should Know

  • 1
    The investors were both victims and participants. Many of Onofrio's clients signed documents containing false information — coached or not. Federal prosecutors in similar cases have charged co-conspirators at every level of the chain. Being coached to lie doesn't automatically mean immunity from liability.
  • 2
    Temporary wire transfers used to fake down payments left the clearest forensic trail. Moving money into an account solely to produce a bank statement showing required capital — then pulling it after closing — creates a transaction record that investigators can reconstruct precisely. This tactic has been the thread that unraveled multiple similar schemes nationwide.
  • 3
    Loan defaults triggered the investigation, not a whistleblower. The scheme collapsed when investors couldn't service loans taken out on inflated properties. Banks holding these notes noticed the pattern across their portfolios. Commercial lenders losing money on bad loans became, effectively, the investigative starting point.
  • 4
    A media profile isn't due diligence. Podcast appearances and LinkedIn credibility don't constitute vetting. The same channels that made Onofrio appear legitimate were the recruiting pipeline for the fraud. Anyone asking you to misrepresent anything on a financial application — regardless of their credentials — is asking you to commit bank fraud.

Updates

As of December 2025, Onofrio has been sentenced and is awaiting report to federal custody. Court records related to the case are on file in the District of Minnesota. Separate civil proceedings related to lender losses were anticipated by legal observers but had not been filed publicly at the time of sentencing.

The case was announced by U.S. Attorney Daniel N. Rosen for the District of Minnesota.
