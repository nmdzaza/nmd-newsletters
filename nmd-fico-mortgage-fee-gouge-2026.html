FICO Just Hit Homebuyers With a Hidden $99 Fee — $198 Per Couple

FICO doubled its mortgage credit check price with a new "penalty" fee model. Costs went from $15 to $115 per person — an 8x jump baked into every home purchase application. The Mortgage Bankers Association called it a government-granted oligopoly abuse. Here's what changed and what you can actually do about it.

By NMD ZAZA 🐐 March 16, 2026 6 min read

You're trying to buy a house. You've repaired your credit. You've saved the down payment. You show up to the lender — and before you even get a rate quote, FICO is pulling $99 out of your pocket through a fee structure that didn't exist two years ago. If your spouse is on the application, make it $198.

This isn't a lender fee. It's not a bank fee. It's a direct extraction from homebuyers embedded in the mortgage origination process — quietly added to your closing costs by a company that controls the scoring model that 90% of mortgage lenders are required to use. And it just doubled.

⚠ Cost Alert

FICO raised mortgage score pricing from $4.95 to $10 per score for 2026. Add bureau reseller fees and the total consumer hit per person jumped from ~$15 to as high as $115. The Mortgage Bankers Association estimates industry-wide costs increase by $400 million — and that bill gets passed to buyers.

What FICO actually did

Here's the structure of the new pricing model that went into effect for 2026: FICO raised its direct license price from $4.95 per score to $10 per score — a clean 2x increase. That alone would have been an industry irritant. But the real damage came from what FICO layered on top of it.

FICO introduced a new "Mortgage Direct License" program with a $99 penalty fee built in per consumer applicant. The fee is triggered when a lender pulls credit for a mortgage origination and FICO decides the lender hasn't licensed their scores directly. The net result: a $99 assessment per individual borrower, $198 per couple co-purchasing a home.

Combined with existing bureau reseller fees — which also increased — the total cost of a mortgage credit check that used to run $15 per person is now hitting $115. That's an 8x increase that shows up as a line item somewhere in your mortgage paperwork, often buried in third-party origination fees.

Why this matters beyond the dollar amount

The fee issue is just the most visible part of a broader power play happening right now in the mortgage credit score market. FICO is also rolling out FICO 10T — a new scoring model with different logic than the Classic FICO scores lenders have used for decades. The two changes are related: FICO is using the model transition as leverage to restructure how it charges for scores.

The old model was simple: lenders paid bureaus for tri-merge credit reports, bureaus paid FICO a licensing fee embedded in the report cost, and that got passed to consumers as a modest origination fee. Under the new structure, FICO is billing lenders directly — and threatening penalty fees when lenders try to route around the new pricing through existing bureau agreements.

That penalty is the $99 fee. It's not technically charged to the consumer — but it's charged to the lender, who then passes it to the consumer through closing costs. The MBA said it publicly: this is an abuse of market position by a company that has no real competition in the mortgage score space, because Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac mandate its use.

ℹ Context

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have been transitioning to VantageScore 4.0 and FICO 10T as required scores — but that transition is still in progress. Until it fully rolls out, FICO Classic scores remain required for GSE-backed mortgages. FICO knows this. The pricing change came right at this transition window.

The VantageScore counter-move — and why it matters for your credit file

Experian and TransUnion saw this coming. In response to FICO's price hike, Experian announced it will offer VantageScore 4.0 at $0.99 per mortgage origination score — a deliberate counter-move to undercut FICO's leverage during the transition period. TransUnion followed with a similar price cut.

This is bigger than just pricing competition. VantageScore 4.0 uses different scoring logic than FICO — it incorporates rent, utilities, and phone payment history into the model. For people who've been building their credit profile with on-time rent payments, this is a significant advantage. FICO models don't capture that data the same way.

Additionally, Fannie Mae eliminated its minimum credit score requirement in November 2025. That means lenders using VantageScore 4.0 can now potentially approve borrowers who didn't qualify under the old 620 FICO minimum — and at $0.99 per score versus $10+ for FICO, the economics strongly favor the switch.

The question is how fast the market transitions. Lenders are conservative. Many will keep pulling FICO Classic until GSE requirements formally mandate the new models — which is expected to roll out progressively through 2026.

The real fight — who controls the mortgage score monopoly

The FICO pricing move is an opening shot in what's becoming a genuine market war. On one side: FICO, which has held a near-monopoly on mortgage credit scoring for 30+ years because GSEs required it. On the other: the credit bureaus themselves (Experian, TransUnion, Equifax), which created VantageScore as a direct competitor to reduce their dependence on FICO licensing fees.

The bureaus have been pushing for VantageScore adoption in mortgages for years. The FHFA's 2022 ruling that approved FICO 10T and VantageScore 4.0 for GSE-backed mortgages was a major win. Now with the pricing squeeze from FICO, the bureaus have even more motivation to accelerate the transition.

For consumers, this is actually positive long-term. Competition in credit scoring models means more borrowers get approved, alternative data gets counted, and the 620 FICO cliff becomes less of a gatekeeping mechanism. But in the short term — right now, in 2026 — FICO still controls the game, and they are squeezing every dollar out of their position before the transition completes.

5 moves to make right now

  • 1
    Ask your lender which scoring model they use. Some lenders — especially credit unions and non-bank mortgage lenders — have already migrated to VantageScore 4.0. If your VantageScore is higher than your FICO (it often is for rent payers), this could mean a lower rate or easier approval without paying the FICO penalty fee structure.
  • 2
    Add rent to your credit file before applying. Experian Boost, Rental Kharma, and RentReporters all report rent history to at least one bureau. With VantageScore 4.0 now viable for mortgages, this is no longer just a credit score trick — it's mortgage qualification strategy. Do this 60-90 days before your target application date.
  • 3
    Rate-shop within a 45-day window. Under FICO 10T and VantageScore 4.0, multiple mortgage inquiries within 45 days of each other count as a single inquiry. This is an expanded window from the old 14-day deduplication window. Use it — apply to multiple lenders in a concentrated period to find the best rate without multiplying your inquiry impact.
  • 4
    Request a fee itemization on every loan estimate. FICO's penalty fee doesn't always appear as a labeled line item — it gets bundled into "credit report" or "third-party services" fees. Ask your loan officer specifically: "What credit reporting fees are in this estimate and why?" If they can't explain a $99+ charge, push back.
  • 5
    Build the trended data profile FICO 10T rewards. FICO 10T uses "trended data" — it looks at whether your balances are going up or down over time, not just what they are today. Consistently paying down balances (not just maintaining them) scores significantly better. If you're 6+ months from buying, use that window to build a downward utilization trend across your accounts.

Bottom line

FICO has a monopoly, knows it, and just used it. The $99 fee is real, it's in your closing costs, and it's not going away until VantageScore 4.0 fully displaces FICO in the GSE framework. That transition is coming — but not fast enough to help buyers closing loans today.

What you can control: know which scoring model your lender uses, add every piece of positive payment history you can to your file, rate-shop in the 45-day window, and push back on unexplained fees in your loan estimate. The market is changing in buyers' favor — just not fast enough for 2026 to be painless.
