FTC Is Mailing $10.9 Million in Checks to Victims of a Credit Repair Pyramid Scheme

The feds finally caught up to Financial Education Services — a company that charged people hundreds of dollars to "fix" their credit while secretly running a pyramid scheme underneath. 443,048 victims are getting checks. If you or someone you know paid them, read this now.

Aye, let me give you the full picture on this one because it matters — and because the lesson inside of it is bigger than just one company.

The Federal Trade Commission just announced it's sending out $10.9 million in refund checks to nearly half a million consumers who got taken by Financial Education Services — also known as FES, United Wealth Education, United Credit Education Services, and Youth Financial Literacy Foundation. If you've heard of any of those names, pay attention.

Here's how the scam worked. FES targeted people with damaged credit. They pitched a credit repair program that sounded legitimate — pay $99 upfront, then up to $89 per month for ongoing services. The promise was simple: we'll fix your credit. The reality? The services barely worked, if they worked at all. The FTC's investigation found that FES was charging people real money for results they weren't delivering.

But here's where it gets worse. Underneath the credit repair pitch was a full pyramid scheme. FES recruited its own customers to become "agents" and sign up new customers. Agents earned commissions on recruiting — not on actually fixing anyone's credit. The more people you brought in, the more money flowed upward. The company pulled in over $213 million before the FTC shut it down.

They sued in 2022. Settlements came in 2024 with permanent bans for the owners and operators. And now in March 2026, the FTC is actually cutting checks — 443,048 of them — to the people who got burned. The refund administrator number is 833-699-7995 if you or anyone you know needs to check on a claim. Recipients have 90 days to cash their checks.

Now here's the real talk. This isn't just a story about one bad company. This is about a pattern that still exists in the credit repair industry right now. Every week there are companies running similar models — charging monthly fees, delivering nothing, and wrapping it all in MLM recruiting structures where the only people making money are at the top.

Here's how to spot a credit repair scam before you pay:

1 — They promise specific results before seeing your report. No legitimate service can guarantee a specific score increase without looking at your actual situation. If someone promises "we'll add 100 points" before they've seen anything — that's a red flag.

2 — They charge upfront before doing any work. The Credit Repair Organizations Act (CROA) prohibits credit repair companies from charging you before they perform services. If they want money upfront — that's a federal violation.

3 — They pressure you to recruit others. The moment a "credit repair" company starts telling you to sign up your friends and family and earn commissions on their fees — you're in a pyramid scheme, not a credit service.

4 — They can't tell you exactly what they're disputing or why. Real credit repair means reviewing your actual report, identifying specific inaccurate or unverifiable items, and disputing those items with documented evidence. Vague promises and generic templates are a waste of your money.

5 — They guarantee to remove accurate negative information. If something is accurate and verifiable — a real late payment, a real collection — no one can legally guarantee its removal. Anyone who says otherwise is lying to you.
