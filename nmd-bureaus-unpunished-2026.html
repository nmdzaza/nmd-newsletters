What ProPublica just exposed

On March 11, 2026, ProPublica and CNN published an investigation that confirmed what everyone in credit repair already felt but couldn't prove with hard numbers: the bureaus stopped resolving your disputes the moment the CFPB stopped watching.

The data is brutal. Experian resolved roughly 20% of consumer complaints in its favor in 2024 — not great, but at least real. By 2025, that number cratered to less than 1%. Less than one percent. TransUnion's relief rate — which had held relatively steady for years — began plunging in the summer of 2025 and was cut roughly in half by October. The third bureau, Equifax, held its rates up — but only because it's locked into a consent order it signed with the CFPB just before the administration changed.

⚠ What This Means For You

Filing a complaint with the CFPB no longer triggers any real pressure on the bureaus. Experian and TransUnion know the enforcement teeth are gone. Your dispute letter alone — without legal backing — has less leverage than it did 18 months ago.

Why Equifax is the outlier

Here's the part most outlets aren't emphasizing enough: Equifax didn't just get lucky. Days before Trump's inauguration, Equifax signed a formal CFPB consent order that locked them into mandatory dispute reforms and ongoing federal oversight — regardless of who runs the agency. They're stuck doing the right thing by contract. Experian and TransUnion had no such agreement in place. That gap is everything.

"The thing that is making them do any kind of effort is a lawsuit or a regulator, and now we don't have the regulator."

— Chi Chi Wu, National Consumer Law Center

Real people. Real damage.

This isn't abstract. ProPublica profiled real cases showing the human cost of what the data is telling us.

Rebecca Sheppard has been trying for years to remove a fraudulent $240,000 student loan from her credit file — a loan she never took out. The error has blocked her from purchasing an accessible home for her disabled father. Despite repeated disputes and CFPB complaints, nothing has moved.

Kwami Abdul-Bey, an Air Force veteran, had his entire mortgage payment history erroneously deleted from his credit report. Without it, his score tanked, blocking him from refinancing and saving hundreds of dollars a month. The bureau acknowledged the error. They still haven't fixed it.

💡 The Pattern

Both cases share the same structure: legitimate dispute, bureau acknowledgment, no action. When there's no federal pressure to comply, compliance becomes optional. And right now, it is.

What changed — and why

In February 2025, Russell Vought — a White House budget official — took over the CFPB as acting director. He froze nearly all agency activity: paused investigations, dropped lawsuits (including an active case against TransUnion), and began mass layoffs of agency staff.

The CFPB still exists on paper. The laws — FCRA, FDCPA, ECOA — haven't changed. But enforcement has evaporated. And without enforcement, the bureaus are making a calculated business decision: why spend money resolving complaints if no one's going to make you?

What you need to do right now

The playbook has shifted. Here's how to protect yourself in the no-enforcement era:

  • 1
    Pull all three reports immediately. Go to AnnualCreditReport.com. Download PDFs. Screenshot everything. You need a timestamp of exactly what's on your file today before conditions get worse.
  • 2
    Dispute in writing via certified mail — not online. Online portals let bureaus dismiss disputes faster. A paper dispute with USPS certified tracking creates a legal record. Keep copies of everything. If they fail to respond within 30 days, you have grounds for a lawsuit.
  • 3
    File with your state AG, not just the CFPB. Many state attorneys general are actively filling the enforcement gap the federal government left. Illinois, California, New York, and Connecticut AGs have signaled they're watching bureau conduct. A state complaint can trigger action the CFPB won't.
  • 4
    Find a consumer rights attorney for FCRA violations. Under the FCRA, if a bureau fails to conduct a reasonable investigation, you can sue in federal court and potentially recover actual damages, statutory damages ($100–$1,000 per violation), and attorney's fees. Attorneys often take these cases on contingency. The lawsuit is the new CFPB complaint.
  • 5
    Document everything toward Equifax first. Given the consent order, Equifax is your best chance at getting errors corrected through standard channels right now. Save Experian and TransUnion disputes for the legal route.
✅ Silver Lining

Consumer attorneys report an uptick in FCRA lawsuit filings — and winning. Courts are still enforcing the law even when the CFPB isn't. The legal threat is now your most powerful dispute tool.
