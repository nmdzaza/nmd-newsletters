676 Million SSNs.
Your number is probably in it.

The alleged Infutor breach just put 676 million American consumer records — including Social Security numbers, dates of birth, and addresses — on the dark web. This isn't a drill. Here's exactly what you do right now.

📅 March 17, 2026 ✎ NMD ZAZA ⏱ 8 min read

676 million records. That's more than twice the U.S. adult population.

A threat actor known as "Spirigatito" is claiming to have breached Infutor — a consumer identity data company owned by Verisk Analytics — and dumped 676,798,866 consumer records onto dark web marketplaces. The database allegedly includes full names, dates of birth, physical addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, and Social Security numbers.

To put that number in context: there are about 260 million adults in the United States. This database is allegedly more than 2.6 times that size — meaning your information could appear in it multiple times across different data points, different addresses, different versions of your identity profile over the years.

Infutor — now operating under Verisk Analytics — is one of the largest consumer identity management companies in the U.S. Their data is used by insurance companies, mortgage lenders, auto finance firms, and real estate operators to verify identities and assess risk. In other words, they hold the identity infrastructure that the financial system relies on. And according to investigators, it was sitting exposed on a misconfigured Elasticsearch database.

⚠ Breaking Alert — March 2026

Infutor has not yet officially confirmed the breach. Class action investigations have already launched at ClassAction.org. The data is allegedly already circulating on dark web forums. Do not wait for an official notification letter — those take months to arrive and the damage happens in days. Act now.

Verisk Analytics is a publicly traded data analytics giant. Their Infutor subsidiary describes itself as helping businesses "verify identities, reduce fraud, and improve marketing." The irony is brutal — a company built to prevent fraud may have just enabled the largest mass identity theft event in American history.

The breach was traced to a misconfigured Elasticsearch database — a cloud storage system that was left exposed to the public internet without password protection. This is the same class of vulnerability behind other massive breaches like the 2020 BlueKai exposure. It's not a sophisticated hack — it's negligence. And millions of Americans pay the price.

Who is Spirigatito — and what happens to your data

Spirigatito is a known threat actor in the dark web data broker community. They don't just hold data — they sell it. Consumer identity packages get carved up, sorted by state, by age range, by credit score tier, and sold to fraudsters who specialize in different types of identity crime.

Why this breach hits harder than the others

You've probably gotten used to data breach notifications. Maybe you've received three this year already. But the Infutor breach is different from a retail loyalty program getting hacked or a hospital losing billing records. Here's why:

Infutor is an identity backbone company. Their entire product is knowing who you are — addresses going back 20 years, phone numbers attached to your SSN, email addresses linked to your identity. This isn't just "your name and email." This is a complete identity dossier assembled specifically to verify and profile you for financial transactions.

When this data lands in criminal hands, fraudsters aren't working with fragments. They're working with the same depth of information your bank uses to verify you. They can answer security questions. They can pass knowledge-based authentication. They can defeat the verification systems designed to catch them.

The NMD action plan — move today, not tomorrow

Every hour of delay increases the probability that your data has already been packaged and sold. Here is the exact sequence, in priority order:

  • 1 Freeze your credit at all five reporting agencies — not just the big three. Equifax.com, Experian.com, and TransUnion.com are obvious. But also freeze at Innovis (innovis.com) and NCTUE (nctue.com), which covers utility and telecom accounts. A freeze at only Equifax/Experian/TransUnion still leaves backdoors open. Do all five. It's free. It takes 10 minutes per bureau. Do it right now, before you finish reading this.
  • 2 Get an IRS Identity Protection PIN before tax season closes. Go to IRS.gov/ippin. This PIN must be included on your federal tax return and prevents anyone else from filing in your name. With SSN data from the Infutor breach already in circulation, tax fraud is the fastest-moving threat. The PIN is free and takes 15 minutes to set up. This one move can save you months of IRS nightmare.
  • 3 Pull all three credit reports and scan for anything new. Go to AnnualCreditReport.com. You're entitled to free weekly reports. Print or screenshot them today as a baseline. Look for: any new accounts you didn't open, hard inquiries from lenders you never contacted, new addresses attached to your file, and any accounts showing as "transferred" or "sold." Any anomaly needs a certified mail dispute immediately.
  • 4 Place a fraud alert at one bureau — it propagates to all three. A fraud alert requires lenders to contact you and verify your identity before opening new accounts. Unlike a freeze, it doesn't block access — it triggers a verification call. Use both: freeze for blocking, fraud alert for the early warning layer. Place it at Equifax, Experian, or TransUnion — they're legally required to notify the other two.
  • 5 File a report at IdentityTheft.gov if you see fraudulent activity. The FTC's IdentityTheft.gov portal generates an official recovery plan and creates an identity theft report that carries legal weight with creditors and credit bureaus. This document is what you'll need to dispute fraudulent accounts and override creditor resistance. Do not skip this step if you find anything wrong.
  • 6 Check ChexSystems and LexisNexis for banking and background record fraud. Fraudsters use stolen SSNs to open bank accounts and pass background checks. Request your free ChexSystems report at ChexSystems.com and your LexisNexis consumer file at LexisNexisRisk.com/consumers. These records affect your ability to open bank accounts and pass tenant screening — they're often ignored until the damage is already done.

The system is failing — and NMD fills the gap

Let's be direct: the data broker industry has zero federal regulation worth mentioning. Companies like Infutor and Verisk collect, aggregate, and sell your personal information at industrial scale with almost no oversight. A bipartisan bill (the American Data Privacy and Protection Act) has been bouncing around Congress for years. It has not passed. Meanwhile, your SSN sits in a misconfigured cloud bucket.

The CFPB — which at least had the authority to investigate data broker abuses — has been stripped down. The FTC has jurisdiction but limited enforcement bandwidth. State attorneys general are fighting back, but it's a patchwork. The systemic protection you'd need simply doesn't exist at scale.

What does exist is individual action backed by the right tools. A credit freeze works. An IRS IP PIN works. Real-time credit monitoring works. AI-powered dispute management works. None of these are passive — they require you to move. But when you move fast, you intercept the fraud before it lands on your report and before it takes 12 to 18 months to clean up.
