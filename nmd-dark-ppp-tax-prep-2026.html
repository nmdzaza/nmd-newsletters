Federal Fraud · March 2026

He Filed 1,500 Fake PPP Loans.
Then Texted: "Most Stupid Fraud in History."

Sharhabeel Shreiteh ran a suburban Chicago tax prep shop that became a $14 million COVID relief fraud factory. He submitted over 1,500 fraudulent applications, pocketed $741K in kickbacks, wired the rest to a secret second wife overseas — and left a text message trail that prosecutors called gift-wrapped.

📅 March 25, 2026 ✍️ Za | NMD ZAZA ⏰ 6 min read

From tax prep office to federal fraud factory.

In 2020, as Congress rushed billions in COVID-19 relief money out the door with minimal oversight, Sharhabeel Shreiteh saw an opportunity. The 46-year-old tax preparer from Crete, Illinois had an existing client base — sole proprietors, self-employed workers, small business owners — people who trusted him with their financial information. He used every piece of it.

Shreiteh began filing fraudulent Paycheck Protection Program applications on behalf of his clients — and in some cases, without their knowledge. He inflated income figures. He fabricated payroll records. He built applications designed to maximize the loan amounts the SBA would approve. And he kept going, month after month, until the machine had processed more than 1,500 fraudulent PPP loan applications totaling approximately $14 million in approved COVID relief funds — virtually all of it later forgiven, meaning taxpayers absorbed the full loss.

For each fraudulent application, Shreiteh collected a kickback — a cut of whatever his clients received. By the time investigators began tracing the money, he had pocketed approximately $741,000 in direct kickback payments. That was just what hit his accounts in Illinois. The rest of the story was playing out overseas.

The villa, the Mercedes, and the second wife.

While this was happening, Shreiteh's American wife was texting him about financial pressures at home. What she didn't know — and what investigators eventually uncovered — was that her husband had been systematically wiring hundreds of thousands of dollars internationally to Palestine, where he maintained a second family.

The overseas transfers funded a lifestyle that stood in sharp contrast to his suburban Illinois life: a villa purchased in Palestine, a Mercedes-Benz for his secret second wife, and ongoing financial support for the children he had with her. The money trail wound through wire transfers that IRS Criminal Investigation agents spent months tracing back to the fraudulent PPP loans flowing through his tax prep office.

⚠ The Numbers

$14 million in fraudulent PPP loans approved. $741,000+ in kickbacks pocketed in Illinois. An unknown additional amount wired internationally. Over 1,500 fraudulent applications filed. All forgiven by the SBA — paid for by American taxpayers.

The texts that sealed it.

Federal fraud investigations don't always produce slam-dunk prosecutions. Paper trails can be obscured. Witnesses go silent. Financial flows become hard to trace. This case wasn't like that.

In communications with an associate, Shreiteh had been unusually candid about what he was doing. Investigators obtained text messages in which he appeared to acknowledge the scheme directly. In one exchange, he wrote to his contact: "You gonna put my ass in jail soon." In another message — one that became the headline quote at his federal sentencing — he described the operation himself:

"Most stupid fraud in history."

That line, written by Shreiteh himself, was read aloud in a Chicago federal courtroom on March 10, 2026. U.S. District Judge Martha M. Pacold sentenced him to 120 months — 10 years — in federal prison, believed to be the longest PPP-related sentence handed down in the entire Northern District of Illinois. He was also ordered to forfeit $741,000 to the U.S. government.

A co-defendant, Tracy Mitchell, still faces pending charges related to the same scheme.

How investigators found the thread — and pulled it.

The case was prosecuted by the IRS Criminal Investigation division, which specializes in following financial flows through complex wire transfer networks. The investigation traced Shreiteh's kickback payments back through the fraudulent applications he had filed, built the timeline of the overseas wire transfers, and subpoenaed the text message records that provided the most direct evidence of intent.

The scheme ran from 2020 through what prosecutors described as a sustained, systematic operation — not opportunistic one-off fraud but a deliberate, ongoing enterprise using his tax prep client base as raw material. The scale — 1,500-plus applications — required organization, repetition, and the kind of client trust that a tax preparer uniquely holds.

What You Should Know

  • 1
    PPP fraud prosecutions are still active in 2026. Despite the program ending years ago, federal investigators are still working through the backlog of suspicious applications. The statute of limitations for federal wire fraud is 5 years, and for bank fraud involving financial institutions it extends to 10. Cases filed today can still trace back to 2020.
  • 2
    Text messages are primary evidence in federal fraud cases. Shreiteh's prosecution hinged significantly on his own written communications. Federal investigators routinely subpoena iCloud and carrier records in financial fraud investigations. Anything committed to text, email, or messaging platforms can and will be used.
  • 3
    International wire transfers don't hide money — they create a richer evidence trail. Every wire transfer creates a SWIFT record. The movement of money between U.S. accounts and foreign accounts is flagged under the Bank Secrecy Act and FinCEN reporting requirements. Sending fraud proceeds overseas doesn't obscure the crime — it adds money laundering charges.
  • 4
    Tax preparers hold a unique position of trust — and breach of that trust is a specific aggravating factor at sentencing. Federal sentencing guidelines include enhancements for abuse of a position of trust. A tax preparer who uses client PII without authorization faces enhanced exposure above what a standard fraud defendant would receive.

Updates

Shreiteh was sentenced March 10, 2026 in the Northern District of Illinois by U.S. District Judge Martha M. Pacold. The sentence is believed to be the longest PPP fraud sentence in that district's history. Co-defendant Tracy Mitchell's case remains open. The IRS Criminal Investigation division continues active enforcement of COVID-era fraud claims under a dedicated task force that was extended through at least 2026.
