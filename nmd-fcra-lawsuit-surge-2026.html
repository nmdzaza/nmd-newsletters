FCRA lawsuits exploded 47%.
Credit bureaus are losing in court.

832 lawsuits filed against credit bureaus and furnishers in January 2026 alone — a 47.5% surge from last year. If they ignored your dispute, they violated federal law. Here's exactly what you can do about it.

The numbers don't lie — consumers are fighting back

Something shifted in 2026. Consumers who used to accept inaccurate credit reports as a fact of life are now lawyering up — and winning. FCRA litigation has been climbing for years, but what's happening right now is a different level.

In January 2026 alone, 832 FCRA lawsuits were filed — a 47.5% spike compared to January 2025, and part of a trend that has seen FCRA litigation grow by over 147% since 2014. Credit complaint volumes hit an all-time high of 2 million+ at the CFPB in 2024, and that pressure has now spilled into the federal court system.

The three big bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — are defendants in the majority of these cases. But furnishers (creditors and debt collectors who report inaccurate data) are increasingly on the hook too. The legal ecosystem around consumer credit rights has never been more active.

💡 Why This Matters to You

This isn't just a legal trend. It's proof that the system works when you know how to use it. Every one of those 832 lawsuits started with someone whose dispute was ignored or bungled. The FCRA gave them a weapon — and they used it.

What the FCRA actually gives you

The Fair Credit Reporting Act has been federal law since 1970. Most people know it as "the dispute law." What most people don't know is the teeth behind it.

When you submit a dispute to a credit bureau, they have 30 days to investigate. During that window, they must contact the furnisher (the company that reported the information), conduct a reasonable investigation, and either correct, delete, or verify the item. If they can't verify it — it has to come off.

Here's where it gets powerful: if the bureau fails to follow this process — ignores your dispute, reinvestigates sloppily, or fails to delete information that can't be verified — they've violated federal law. And the FCRA gives you a private right of action to sue them directly in federal district court.

"The FCRA is one of the few consumer protection laws where the plaintiff can collect attorney's fees from the defendant. That's why lawyers take these cases — and why the bureaus are paying out millions."

What you can collect in a successful FCRA lawsuit:

Actual Damages Loan denials, higher interest rates, job losses tied to inaccurate info Unlimited

That attorney's fee provision is key. It means consumer protection lawyers take FCRA cases on contingency — no money up front — because if they win, the bureau pays the legal fees. This has created an entire ecosystem of attorneys who specialize in exactly this.

Why the surge is happening right now

Three forces are colliding in 2026 to drive this spike:

1. The CFPB gap. With the CFPB's enforcement power weakened, federal regulators aren't chasing bureaus the way they used to. That leaves private litigation as the primary enforcement mechanism. Consumer law attorneys saw this coming — they're staffed up and filing.

2. Credit awareness is at an all-time high. Apps like Credit Karma, Experian's free monitoring, and AI-powered dispute tools have put millions of people face-to-face with their credit files for the first time. More eyes on reports means more errors discovered. More errors discovered means more disputes filed. More disputes ignored means more lawsuits.

3. State laws are layering on top. California, New York, Illinois, and other states have passed their own credit reporting laws that are stricter than the FCRA in some respects — and state attorneys general are aggressively filling the CFPB void. Plaintiffs can sometimes stack state and federal claims, increasing their leverage.

⚠ The Capital One Signal

Capital One recently settled a $2.4 million FCRA class action for violations related to credit reporting. This isn't isolated — major lenders are settling FCRA cases regularly because fighting them to verdict is expensive and unpredictable. The legal risk is real, and smart plaintiffs know it.

The exact moves to make if your dispute was ignored

The system is working — but only for people who work it correctly. Here's the NMD playbook for turning a bureau violation into leverage:

  • 1
    Document the original dispute. Pull your credit report (AnnualCreditReport.com, free weekly). Screenshot every error. Note the exact date you submitted the dispute. If you did it online, save the confirmation. If you didn't — send your next dispute via certified mail. That timestamped receipt is your evidence.
  • 2
    Mark your calendar for day 30. The bureau has 30 days from receipt of your dispute to respond. Day 31 with no substantive response or verification? That's a potential violation. The clock starts the moment they receive it — certified mail gives you proof of receipt.
  • 3
    File a CFPB complaint regardless. Yes, CFPB enforcement is weaker right now. File anyway. A complaint creates a federal record. If you later file a lawsuit, that complaint log demonstrates a pattern of bureau conduct. Bureau legal teams track complaint ratios — escalation via complaint still carries weight.
  • 4
    Contact a consumer law attorney. After the 30-day window, contact a consumer protection attorney. Search "FCRA attorney [your state]" or use the National Association of Consumer Advocates (NACA) directory at consumeradvocates.org. Most take consultations free. If you have a solid case, they may take it on contingency.
  • 5
    Escalate to your state AG simultaneously. Don't wait on federal channels. Your state attorney general's office may have independent enforcement authority with faster timelines. California, New York, and Illinois in particular have been aggressive. A state AG complaint + federal complaint + private attorney letter is a three-front assault the bureaus take seriously.
