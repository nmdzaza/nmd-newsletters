They Plugged In a Thumb Drive.
The ATM Started Spitting Out Cash.

Tren de Aragua — a Venezuelan gang federally designated as a foreign terrorist organization — deployed Ploutus malware across 1,529 ATMs in the United States, stealing $40.73 million over four years. The DOJ has now charged 87 of them.

📅 December 2025 ✍️ NMD ZAZA ⏰ 6 min read

One thumb drive. One open hood. $40 million.

The operation was almost insultingly simple. Members of Tren de Aragua — a Venezuelan criminal network that the U.S. government has designated as a foreign terrorist organization — would locate an ATM, approach it during off-hours, and open the machine's external panel. If it triggered an alarm, they'd walk. If it didn't, they'd proceed.

Then they'd either swap out the hard drive with one pre-loaded with Ploutus malware, or plug in a thumb drive containing the same program. Ploutus — a variant of malware first observed in Mexico in 2013 — was designed to do one thing: force ATMs to dispense their entire cash cassettes on command. No card. No PIN. Just a connected device and a waiting operative with a bag.

Federal prosecutors say this happened 1,529 times across the United States between 2021 and 2025. Total stolen: $40.73 million. And the proceeds didn't stay in anyone's pocket — according to the DOJ, the money flowed directly to Tren de Aragua leadership to fund what prosecutors called "unspeakably evil acts."

⚠ The Numbers

87 total defendants federally charged. 1,529 jackpotting incidents. $40.73 million stolen. Operations documented across multiple U.S. states. Proceeds routed to TdA leadership for terrorist operations. The alleged mastermind, Hector Rusthenford Guerrero Flores, remains at large with a $5 million U.S. government bounty on his head.

How Tren de Aragua built the operation

Tren de Aragua started as a prison gang in Venezuela's Tocorón Penitentiary and expanded aggressively into Latin America and eventually the United States. By the time federal investigators had fully mapped the ATM jackpotting operation, TdA had established cells in multiple U.S. states — recruiting individuals specifically to run what amounted to a domestic cash extraction program.

The operational playbook was disciplined. Operatives would first conduct reconnaissance — assessing the external security setup at target ATMs, checking for cameras, testing whether opening the hood triggered an immediate alarm or response. Only after passing that check would they proceed with installation. The Ploutus variant they deployed was also engineered to delete its own evidence logs — designed to deceive bank technicians and law enforcement who examined the machines afterward.

When the cash came out, couriers collected it and moved it up the chain. The investigation — eventually expanded under Joint Task Force Vulcan, the same multi-agency effort targeting MS-13 — found that money routed through the network funded TdA's broader criminal and terrorist infrastructure, which includes sex trafficking of minors, kidnapping, murder, extortion, and drug trafficking.

"The breadth and scale of these alleged conspiracies and the strength and skill of our investigators and prosecutors who dismantle them."
— DOJ statement on the 87-defendant indictment, December 2025

The indictments

The case was built across multiple federal districts. Two separate indictments were returned in Nebraska:

The first, on October 21, 2025, charged 32 individuals with conspiracy to commit bank fraud, computer fraud, and multiple counts of bank burglary.

The second, on December 9, 2025, charged an additional 22 people with bank fraud, burglary, and money laundering.

Additional defendants were charged in Colorado — including two suspected group leaders on RICO offenses — and in New York, New Mexico, and Texas. The combined total reached 87 charged defendants by mid-December 2025.

The alleged mastermind behind the operation, Hector Rusthenford Guerrero Flores, was charged separately in New York and is currently believed to be outside the United States. The U.S. government has placed a $5 million bounty on his capture. He was previously sanctioned by the U.S. government in July 2025. Another named leader, Jimena Romina Araya Navarro, was also charged as part of the broader indictments.

What the charges carry

Defendants face a range of federal charges depending on their role in the operation. Bank fraud conspiracy carries up to 30 years per count. Computer fraud charges carry up to 10 years. Bank burglary convictions carry maximums up to 20 years. Money laundering charges add an additional 20 years per count. The DOJ noted that convicted defendants collectively face maximum sentences ranging from 20 to 335 years in federal prison, depending on the specific charges each individual faces.

The RICO counts filed against the Colorado defendants carry up to 20 years on their own and open the door to forfeiture of all proceeds connected to the racketeering enterprise.

How they were caught

ATM jackpotting leaves a physical and digital footprint that investigators can work backward from. Hard drive replacements and thumb drive connections are logged in machine diagnostics. Surveillance cameras at banks and nearby businesses captured footage of operatives during reconnaissance and installation. The Ploutus malware variant, while designed to erase evidence from the ATM itself, still produced identifiable artifacts that forensic examiners could link across incidents.

Financial investigators traced the cash movement. The sheer volume of incidents — 1,529 documented attacks over four years — also meant the pattern became undeniable across multiple jurisdictions. Joint Task Force Vulcan, which had already been monitoring TdA's U.S. operations in connection with other crimes, used the ATM operation as one thread in a much larger prosecution strategy targeting the gang's full infrastructure in the country.

What You Should Know

  • 1
    Ploutus malware has been active since 2013 and continues to evolve. The variant deployed by TdA was specifically modified to delete its own evidence trail. Financial institutions have known about this attack vector for over a decade — yet 1,529 successful incidents in four years suggests widespread gaps in physical ATM security protocols at banks and credit unions nationwide.
  • 2
    The prosecution linked ATM theft directly to terrorist financing. This is not a typical fraud case. The DOJ's framing — connecting the jackpotting proceeds to TdA's broader operation including sex trafficking, kidnapping, and murder — means prosecutors pursued this under a terrorism-adjacent legal framework. That framing enables harsher sentences and broader asset forfeiture powers.
  • 3
    The mastermind remains at large. Hector Rusthenford Guerrero Flores is charged, sanctioned by the U.S. government, and has a $5 million bounty — and is still free. The 87 indictments represent the operational network. The head of the snake is still out there, and prosecutors have been explicit about that gap in the case.
  • 4
    Reconnaissance was built into the playbook. TdA operatives didn't walk up and install malware blind. They tested alarms. They assessed cameras. They walked away if the security held. That discipline — treating each ATM as an intelligence target before a financial one — is what allowed the operation to run for four years across multiple states without an early bust.

Updates

As of December 2025, prosecutions are underway across multiple federal districts. The 87 charged defendants are in various stages of arraignment and pretrial proceedings. Joint Task Force Vulcan, which was formally expanded in February 2025 to prioritize TdA alongside MS-13, continues active operations targeting remaining TdA infrastructure in the United States.

Hector Rusthenford Guerrero Flores remains at large. The State Department's Rewards for Justice program is offering the $5 million bounty for information leading to his arrest.
